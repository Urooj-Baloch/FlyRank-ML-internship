Which Pages Are Losing Clicks They Should Be Getting? A CTR Opportunity Study

Abstract. Among visible, well-ranked pages, click-through rate (CTR) varies sharply by search position — but does it vary in ways a reviewer can act on? Using a 132,471-page slice of FlyRank's internship search-performance warehouse (March 2026), I built a transparent baseline rule that ranks pages by how far their CTR sits below their own position tier's average, and compared it against a trained Random Forest under an honest, client-grouped validation split. The headline result: the simple, transparent baseline performed at least as well as — and slightly better than — the trained model at larger ranking depths (precision@200/500). This paper reports a ranked, decision-support action queue for content reviewers, built on the baseline rule, along with its explicit limits.

Introduction

A content reviewer with limited time cannot manually check every page in a large site. They need a ranked list: which pages, out of thousands, are most worth reviewing first for a possible click-through problem? A naive approach — flagging any page under a fixed CTR threshold — fails immediately, because CTR mechanically drops with search position: a page in position 20 will almost always have lower CTR than a page in position 2, regardless of its actual quality. The real question is comparative: is this page underperforming relative to its peers at the same position?

Data

This study uses the FlyRank internship-warehouse dataset (build v20260703), specifically the fact_content_daily_performance table (page-day grain) joined to dim_content (content metadata). The working window is March 2026 — a mid-panel month, deliberately not the final month in the dataset, which is reserved as a sealed test window and is the natural outcome period for any past-to-future label.

After filtering to pages with at least 20 impressions (needed to avoid a degenerate, mostly-zero CTR distribution found during data preparation), the working set is 132,471 pages.

Excluded, and why:

  • trend_direction, trend_pct — not present in this table, and would be leakage traps (derived from the same outcome being ranked) if they were.
  • Product-decision fields (e.g. health/priority scores) — not shipped in this dataset, by design, to prevent circular results.
  • All identifiers used are pseudonymized hashes, used only for joining and grouping. No client names, URLs, or raw queries appear anywhere in this study.

Methodology

Assumption: a page's expected CTR should be judged relative to its own position tier, not as a flat number — confirmed directly: CTR ranges from roughly 0.38% in the top-3 positions down to 0.13% for deep results, a real and well-supported spread across thousands of pages per tier (Figure 1).

Figure 1. Observed CTR by position tier, March 2026. CTR drops sharply as position tier gets worse — the core justification for a tier-relative rule instead of a flat threshold.

Baseline: a transparent rule, ctr_underperformer_for_tier, scoring each page by how far its CTR sits below its own position tier's average. Pages with exactly 0% CTR despite strong position and real traffic are flagged separately for a tracking check before a content fix, since this pattern is unusual enough to suggest a possible measurement issue rather than a content one.

Label: a page is labeled "low CTR" if its CTR sits below the median CTR of the working set.

Model: a Random Forest classifier using position, impression volume, word count, and content age — none of which are derived from CTR or the label itself.

Validation design: split by client (grouped, not random) — pages from the same client may share hidden characteristics, so a random split risks the model faking skill by memorizing per-client patterns rather than learning a general rule.

Leakage checks performed: base rate reported alongside every score; a deliberate label-derived-column trap (adding CTR itself as a feature, confirming the score jumps toward 1.0 as expected); a with/without test on suspect features; a random-vs-grouped split comparison, honestly reported even where the result was inconclusive.

At small K, both methods tie at a perfect score — an artifact of test-set size, not a meaningful result. At K=200 and K=500, a real gap appears: the baseline stays perfect while the trained model slips slightly (0.978–0.995). The model's top feature by permutation importance is impression volume, consistent with the baseline's own use of volume as a tie-breaker — the two methods are, in effect, leaning on similar signal, just packaged differently.

Limitations

  • Cross-sectional, not causal. This is a single-month snapshot, not an experiment. It cannot say that editing a flagged page will increase its CTR — only that the page currently looks worth reviewing.
  • Single time window. Built on March 2026 only; a different month could shift which pages qualify.
  • Validation stability. A single grouped-split check is not fully stable evidence on its own; a stronger re-validation would average across multiple folds.
  • Selection bias risk. If reviewers only ever act on the top of this queue, any future "reviewed vs. unreviewed" comparison would be biased by that choice itself.
  • No algorithm claims. Nothing here claims to model or predict how any search engine's ranking algorithm works — only observed, portfolio-level patterns in this dataset.
  • Content-format mismatch is undetected. This method can flag a CTR gap but cannot tell "bad title" apart from "wrong content format for this query" — that judgment needs a human reviewer.

Ranked Recommendations

The action queue ranks 50,752 visible, well-positioned, underperforming pages, each carrying a reason code and one of two actions:

  • review_title_meta (43,449 pages) — review the page's title, meta description, or snippet for a possible click-through improvement.
  • check_tracking_then_review_title_meta (7,303 pages) — pages with exactly 0% CTR despite strong position and real traffic; check analytics/tracking setup before assuming a content problem.

What should never be automated: no page should be auto-edited or auto-published from this list. No page should be removed or de-indexed based on this queue alone — exclusion from the queue reflects the filters used to build it, not a judgment that a page is worthless.
